01

Setup: build the stroke before you make it

Putting is the one part of golf where a beginner can copy a tour player almost exactly, because the stroke is small and the setup does most of the work. Start with the ball: place it a fraction forward of the centre of your stance, about one ball-width, so the putter reaches it just after the bottom of its arc and strikes very slightly upward. Your feet are about shoulder-width apart, your weight is even or a touch on the lead foot, and it stays there. Nothing below the hips moves for the rest of the stroke.

Now bend from the hips until your eyes are directly over the ball or just inside it, never outside. Check it by holding a second ball at the bridge of your nose and dropping it: it should land on the ball or an inch inside. From there let the arms hang so the putter soles flat on the grass, then look down the line and see the shaft and your forearms forming one straight line. That line is what lets the shoulders swing the putter without the wrists adding anything.

Hold the putter lightly, about a three out of ten, in the palms rather than the fingers so the wrists are less inclined to hinge. The face is square to your start line, not to the hole, and a line drawn on the ball makes that easy to see. If you play left-handed, mirror all of it: your lead side is your right side. Take a moment before every putt to run the order, ball, eyes, forearms, grip, face, and the stroke becomes the easy part.

  • Ball one ball-width forward of centre, weight even or slightly on the lead foot.
  • Eyes over the ball or just inside it; test it by dropping a ball from your nose.
  • Shaft and forearms on one line, grip pressure about three out of ten.
  • Face square to the start line, which is seldom the hole.

02

The pendulum stroke

The stroke is a pendulum hung from the shoulders. The shoulders rock, the arms and hands move as a triangle that never changes shape, and the wrists stay quiet from start to finish. Think of the lead shoulder going down and the trail shoulder coming up on the backswing, then the reverse. The putter will trace a slight arc and the face will open and close a little with it; do not fight that, it is what a shoulder pendulum does. What must not happen is any independent movement of the hands, the head or the lower body.

Face angle at impact is almost the whole story of where a putt starts. With a putter the ball leaves within a fraction of a degree of the face, so a face open by 2 degrees on a 10-foot putt starts the ball about 4 inches offline, which is the width of the hole. Path matters far less: a path 2 degrees out-to-in with a square face barely moves the start line. So when a putt misses left or right, look at the face first, and when you practise, practise returning the face square.

Distance comes from the length of the stroke, not from how hard you hit. Keep the rhythm the same on every putt, a steady one-two count with the through-stroke slightly longer than the backswing, and change only how far the putter travels back. A longer stroke at the same rhythm is a faster putter at the ball. Accelerate through, hold the finish with the putter low and pointing down the line, and keep your eyes on the spot where the ball was until you hear it drop or see it stop.

Feel it

Feel the grip resting against the inside of your lead forearm the whole way through, back and forward. If that contact changes at any point, the wrists hinged and the face moved with them.

03

The launch: a slight upward strike and true roll

A putter carries 3 to 4 degrees of loft because the ball sits in a small depression on the green and needs lifting out of it before it can roll. The ideal strike is very slightly upward, a degree or so, with the hands level with or just ahead of the ball so the face delivers 1 to 2 degrees of effective loft. The ball launches a fraction above the grass, skids for roughly the first 10 to 20 percent of its journey, and settles into true forward roll. That roll is what holds a line; a ball still bouncing or skidding is at the mercy of every imperfection.

Two strikes spoil the launch. Hitting down, usually from the ball being too far back or the hands pressed well forward, drives the ball into the turf so it hops and loses speed unpredictably. Hitting up too much, from the ball too far forward or the shoulders tilting back, adds loft and puts the ball in the air with backspin, so it checks before it rolls. A line drawn on the ball is your test: if it rolls end over end without wobbling, the strike was centred and the launch was right.

Strike location matters as much as strike angle. A putt struck half an inch toward the toe loses noticeable speed and, on most putters, starts a little right as the face twists open; toward the heel it starts a little left and also finishes short. Sweet-spot contact is why good putters can trust stroke length for distance, and it is the reason the gate drill below is worth its tedium.

  • Strike a degree or so upward with 1 to 2 degrees of effective loft.
  • The ball skids for the first 10 to 20 percent, then rolls true.
  • A line on the ball that wobbles means an off-centre strike or a twisted face.
04

Pace and the lag zone

Pace is the master skill in putting, and it has a number. A putt that reaches the hole with enough speed to travel 12 to 18 inches past it, had the hole not been there, holds its line through the last, slowest, most breakable foot and still uses nearly the full width of the cup. Slower than that and the ball wanders as it dies. Faster and the hole shrinks: a putt rolling at a 6-foot-past pace can only enter through the centre, and every lip-out leaves a putt you can miss. Picture the ball dying in a foot past the hole, every time.

Three-putts come from pace far more than from line. From 30 feet the goal is not to hole the putt but to finish inside a 3-foot circle around the hole, and the way to do that is to read the distance, choose a stroke length, and commit to it with your eyes on the target. Uphill, plan for the ball to travel further than the flag looks; downhill, plan to land it well short and let the slope finish the job. Tour players hole about half their putts from 8 feet, so a lag that stops within 3 feet has done its job completely.

Calibrate stroke length on the practice green before every round. Find how far a stroke that goes back to the inside of your trail foot rolls the ball on that day's green, then one that reaches the outside of the trail foot, and use those two reference distances to scale everything else. Green speed changes with mowing, moisture and wind, so the same stroke can roll 3 feet further in a dry afternoon than it did at dawn.

  • Ideal pace: enough to finish 12 to 18 inches past the hole.
  • From 30 feet the target is a 3-foot circle, not the cup.
  • Uphill needs more, downhill needs much less, and the green changes through the day.
05

Reading greens: find the low side, then a spot

Every read begins with the low side. Water drains somewhere on every green, so ask where a bucket of water would run, glance at the surrounding fairway and the horizon, and walk the putt with your feet feeling for the tilt. Look from behind the ball, then from behind the hole where the ball will be rolling slowest and breaking most, then from the low side with ball and hole in one frame. Most golfers under-read, and the great majority of missed breaking putts finish on the low side, so if you are torn between two reads, take the bigger one.

Slope is measured in percent, and hole locations are usually cut on 1 to 3 percent; a 2 percent tilt is enough to feel underfoot once you know to look for it. Break scales with three things: the slope, the speed of the green, and the time the ball spends rolling. As a working picture, a 10-foot putt across a 2 percent side slope on a Stimp 10 green breaks somewhere around half a foot, and on 3 percent closer to a foot. Double the slope and the break roughly doubles. Faster greens break more, because the ball rolls slower for longer. Uphill putts break less, downhill putts break more.

The aim-point idea turns that feel into a spot. Stand astride the line a third of the way from the hole, feel which foot carries more weight, and call it a number: 1 for a tilt you can barely sense, 2 for one you can name, 3 for one that makes you brace. Then stand behind the ball, hold up that many fingers at arm's length beside the hole on the high side, and aim at the outside edge of the last finger. The fingers scale break to distance; what matters is committing to a spot beside the hole and rolling the ball at it at the right pace.

06

Practice structures and the on-course routine

Practice needs a structure or it turns into hitting putts at holes. Three drills cover the whole skill. The gate drill trains the face and the strike: two tees just wider than the putter head around the ball, and a second pair just wider than the ball 18 inches down the line. A putt that passes both was struck in the centre with a square face. The ladder drill trains pace: tees every 5 feet from 10 to 40 feet, and a ball lagged to each rung in order, restarting from the first rung whenever a ball finishes more than 2 feet from its tee.

The clock drill trains pressure. Twelve balls in a circle at 3 feet, holed in a row before you leave; miss one and the count starts again. Then a circle at 4 feet, then 5. Tour players treat 3 feet as automatic and 5 feet as a real putt, and the clock shows you why: the same stroke under a running count feels different. Spend a third of each session on each drill and finish with the clock, so you always leave the green having holed your last putt.

On the course, condense all of it into a routine that takes less than 30 seconds. Read from behind the ball and from the low side while others are putting. Pick a spot beside the hole, set the face to it using the line on the ball, take two practice strokes while looking at the hole to feel the distance, then step in, take one look, and go. The routine is not superstition. It is the way the read, the pace and the stroke happen in the same order every time, so that what you practised is what you use.

  • Gate drill for face and strike, ladder drill for pace, clock drill for pressure.
  • Finish every session on the clock so the last putt you hit went in.
  • Two practice strokes looking at the hole, one look, go.

Faults & fixes

Pushes and pulls from short range

You seePutts from 3 to 6 feet start left or right of the intended line even when the pace is good, and misses come in both directions.

BecauseThe face is not square at impact. With a putter the ball starts almost exactly where the face points, so a face 2 degrees open or closed on a 6-foot putt moves the start line about 2.5 inches, enough to catch the lip or miss.

FixGate drill with a line on the ball: set the line at your start line, roll putts through a gate just wider than the putter head, and watch whether the line rolls true. Rehearse the finish with the face still square to the gate.

Wristy stroke

You seeDistance control is erratic; some putts jump off the face and race by, others die short from the same length of stroke.

BecauseThe wrists hinge and unhinge through the stroke, which changes the loft and the face angle at impact from putt to putt. Stroke length no longer predicts ball speed.

FixSlide the grip up so it rests against the inside of your lead forearm, or hold an alignment stick along the forearm and shaft. Keep the contact from setup to finish; if the stick separates from the forearm, the wrists broke.

Deceleration

You seePutts finish short and drift off line in the last foot, especially downhill or on slow greens, and the stroke feels tentative.

BecauseThe backswing is too long for the distance, so the putter slows into the ball. The ball leaves with less speed than intended and spends its last, slowest feet at the mercy of the slope and the grain.

FixShorten the backswing until the through-stroke is clearly longer than it, and hold the finish for two seconds with the putter low and pointing down the line. Use the ladder drill to recalibrate length to distance.

Head chasing the ball

You seeShort putts pulled left of the hole, strikes toward the heel, and a feeling of looking up to see the result before the putter has finished.

BecauseLifting or rotating the head before impact opens the shoulders toward the target, which drags the path and the face left and moves the strike toward the heel.

FixOn putts inside 6 feet, keep your eyes on the spot where the ball sat until you hear it drop. On longer putts, hold the finish and let the ball leave your peripheral vision before you look up.

Under-reading the break

You seeBreaking putts miss on the low side far more often than the high side, and the ball seems to fall away from the hole in its last foot.

BecauseAiming at the apex, or at the hole, instead of at a spot beyond the apex. Gravity acts on the ball for the whole roll, so a putt aimed at the apex is already below the line when it gets there.

FixFeel the slope percent with your feet, hold up that many fingers beside the hole on the high side, and aim at the outside edge of the last finger. When torn between two reads, choose the bigger one and die the ball in at 12 to 18 inches past pace.

Drills

01

Gate drill

20 putts, aiming for 15 clean rolls

Return the face square and strike the centre of the putter.

  1. On a straight 6-foot putt, push two tees into the green just wider than the putter head, one either side of the ball.
  2. Place a second pair of tees just wider than a ball 18 inches down the line toward the hole.
  3. Draw a line on the ball and set it at the hole, then stroke putts so the putter passes through the first gate and the ball rolls through the second.
  4. Any clip of a tee or wobble of the line is a miss; count only clean rolls.
02

Ladder drill

3 full ladders, up and down

Match stroke length to distance on the green you are about to play.

  1. Place tees at 10, 20, 30 and 40 feet from your ball along a fairly flat line.
  2. Roll one ball to each tee in order, trying to stop it within 2 feet of the tee, and note how far back the putter went for each.
  3. Reverse the order, from 40 feet back to 10.
  4. Restart the ladder whenever a ball finishes more than 2 feet from its tee.
03

Clock drill

Complete circles at 3, 4 and 5 feet

Hole short putts under pressure with a full, accelerating stroke.

  1. Set twelve balls in a circle 3 feet from a hole on a gentle slope, so the putts break in every direction.
  2. Hole all twelve in a row using your full routine on each; if you miss, collect the balls and start again.
  3. When you complete the circle, move it out to 4 feet, then 5 feet.
  4. Finish every session on the 3-foot circle so your last putt goes in.
04

Line-roll check

5 true rolls in a row, three times

Confirm a slightly upward, centred strike that produces true forward roll.

  1. Draw a bold line around a ball and set it at a hole 10 feet away on a flat section.
  2. Address the ball a fraction forward of centre with the hands level with the ball and the eyes over it.
  3. Stroke the putt and watch the line: end over end means a centred, correct launch; a wobble or hop means the strike was off-centre or too steep.
  4. Adjust ball position one ball-width at a time until five putts in a row roll true.
