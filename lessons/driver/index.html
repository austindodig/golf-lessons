01

Why the driver is different

Every other full shot is played off the ground, so the club has to be travelling slightly downward to reach the ball. The driver's the exception. The ball sits on a tee, above the turf, so there's nothing stopping you meeting it after the low point of the arc while the head is already on its way up. Hitting slightly up on the ball, a positive attack angle, raises launch and lowers spin, and that combination is what turns clubhead speed into carry. PGA Tour players average about a degree down, favouring a flight they can control. LPGA players average about +3 degrees, and elite amateurs who hit up sit somewhere around +1 to +3.

It's also the longest and lowest-lofted club in the bag. A standard driver is about 45 inches long with 9 to 12 degrees of loft, so the same swing gives you more speed and a shallower arc than an iron, and small face errors turn into big curves because there's so little loft to soften them. You don't need a new swing for it. The turn, the sequence and the release are the ones you built in the full-swing lesson. The setup is what's different, because it moves the low point behind the ball, and so is what you're trying to do, which is make a wide turn instead of compressing the ball.

  • The tee lets you strike the ball after the low point, on the upswing
  • Hitting up raises launch and lowers spin, which is where carry comes from
  • Same swing as the irons, with a setup that moves the low point behind the ball
  • Low loft magnifies face errors, so the face matters even more
02

Setting up to hit up

Four changes to your iron setup move the low point behind the ball. Start with ball position. Play it inside the lead heel, roughly under the lead armpit, so the club reaches it after the bottom of the arc. Then sort the tee height. With the driver soled on the ground behind the ball, about half the ball should sit above the crown, which on most drivers leaves around an inch and a half of tee showing. Tee it too low and you'll hit down on it without meaning to. Tee it too high and a level strike catches the top edge of the face, or the crown.

Next, widen the stance until the insides of your heels are a touch outside your shoulders, so a faster swing has a stable base under it. Last, set the tilt. Bump your lead hip an inch toward the target and let your upper body lean away from it until your head is clearly behind the ball and your lead shoulder sits higher than your trail shoulder. Your hands should be level with the ball or slightly behind it, never pressed ahead the way they are with an iron. Weight is about even, or a little on the trail foot. Left-handers mirror all of this, lead side right and trail side left.

  • Ball inside the lead heel, under the lead armpit
  • Half the ball above the crown when the driver is soled
  • Heels a touch wider than the shoulders
  • Head behind the ball, lead shoulder high, hands level with the ball
Check it

From face-on, the buttons on your shirt should sit behind the ball and the shaft should be vertical or leaning slightly away from the target. If the shaft's leaning toward the target, the low point has crept forward and you'll end up hitting down on it.

03

Width, turn and a ground-up transition

The first move sets the width of the whole swing. Start the club back with the chest, hands and clubhead moving together, and keep the hands as far from your body as they were at address. The clubhead stays low to the ground for the first foot or so and the lead arm stays long without going stiff. That width gives you the shallow, sweeping arc the driver wants. If the takeaway lifts the club, or the hands snatch it inward, the arc narrows and the delivery gets steep, and steep with a driver means spin and a slice.

Then turn fully. The driver needs the biggest backswing you've got, shoulders turned close to 90 degrees to the target line and hips about half that, with pressure moving into the inside of your trail foot rather than rolling onto the outside of it. You'll feel your lead shoulder working under your chin while your head stays behind the ball. The turn is what stores the speed, so don't cut it short just to keep the club in front of you. A full turn with soft arms gives you more speed than a short turn with fast hands, and it's a lot easier to time.

Start down from the ground. Before your arms have finished going back, pressure shifts into the lead foot and the lead hip starts opening toward the target. That sequence, hips then torso then arms then club, lets the club shallow and drop in behind you instead of getting thrown over the top. Through impact keep your head behind the ball and let the trail shoulder work down and under, so the club is on its way up as it reaches the tee. It should feel like you're hitting up toward a high, tall finish with your belt buckle facing the target. Try it with a few slow swings first and you'll feel it in the trail shoulder.

  • Takeaway: chest, hands and club move together, hands wide from the body
  • Top: shoulders near 90 degrees, pressure inside the trail foot
  • Transition: pressure to the lead foot before the arms come down
  • Impact: head behind the ball, trail shoulder down and under, club rising

04

Launch, spin and what the numbers mean

Distance with a driver comes down to ball speed, launch angle and spin, and that's the order they matter in. Ball speed comes from clubhead speed and centre contact. A solid strike gives a smash factor of about 1.48 to 1.50, meaning the ball leaves at nearly one and a half times the speed of the club. The average male amateur swings the driver at around 93 mph. Typical amateur launch conditions turn that into roughly 215 yards of carry, and optimal conditions from the same speed are worth another 15 to 20 yards. When I see that gap on a launch monitor it's nearly always launch and spin. The speed was already there.

The window for most golfers is a launch of 12 to 15 degrees with spin between 2,000 and 2,800 rpm. The typical amateur launches at around 10 or 11 degrees with spin well above 3,000 rpm, because they're hitting slightly down with a lofted face. Hitting down widens the spin loft, which is the gap between attack angle and delivered loft, and spin loft is what creates spin. Hit up and the gap shrinks, so launch goes up, spin comes down, and the ball flies higher, lands flatter and rolls further. Tour players launch about 11 degrees at 2,700 rpm, but they've got 113 mph of club speed to make a lower flight pay off.

Where you hit the face matters as much as how you swing. A driver's centre of gravity sits behind and below the middle of the face, so contact high on the face launches higher and spins less, and contact low on the face launches lower and spins more. Toe strikes tilt the spin axis so the ball draws, and heel strikes make it fade. That's gear effect, and it's why a slightly high, slightly toe-side strike is the longest ball most golfers will ever hit. Spray the face with foot powder for ten balls. You'll learn more about your driving from that than from any swing thought I could give you.

  • Launch 12 to 15 degrees and spin 2,000 to 2,800 rpm is the window for most golfers
  • Hitting up shrinks spin loft, so spin falls and launch rises
  • High-face strikes launch higher and spin less; low-face strikes do the opposite
  • Toe contact draws and heel contact fades, which is gear effect

05

Controlling the curve

The ball flight laws hit the driver harder than any other club. The face at impact sets the starting direction, and with a driver the ball starts about 85 percent of the way toward where the face is pointing. The difference between face and path sets the curve. A face closed to the path bends the ball left, and a face open to the path bends it right. A slice starts left and curves right because the path travels out to in while the face, even though it's closed to the target, is open to that path. A snap hook is the same thing in reverse.

To hit a stock draw, swing the path a few degrees in to out and deliver the face between the path and the target, say a path of +4 degrees with a face of +2. The ball starts right of the target and curves back toward it. For a fade, turn it round, path a few degrees out to in and the face between the path and the target, so the ball starts left and drifts back. Keep the numbers small. A face-to-path difference of a couple of degrees is plenty to shape a tee shot, and at five or more you're heading for the trees.

Pick one shape and own it. A dependable fade or draw that starts on one side of the fairway and finishes in the middle beats a straight ball that could go either way, because it halves your miss. Build it into the setup rather than the downswing. For a draw, aim the body a little right and the face slightly less right, and tee the ball a fraction higher and further forward so the arc is more in to out and more upward at contact. For a fade do the opposite, and swing with the same commitment either way. Then check the strike, because a toe hit will disguise a fade as a draw.

  • The face sets the start line; face-to-path sets the curve
  • Draw: path in to out, face between the path and the target
  • Fade: path out to in, face between the path and the target
  • Pick one shape and build it into the setup, not the downswing
06

Strategy off the tee

The best drivers of the ball I know rarely hit it as far as they can. They put it where the next shot is easy. Start with an honest number, and I mean the distance you carry with a normal swing rather than your longest drive, because carry is what decides whether a bunker or a creek is in play. Fairways are commonly 30 to 40 yards wide, and even good players miss their line by several yards either side, so think of your target as a corridor rather than a point and choose the corridor with the least trouble along it.

Use your shape. Tee the ball on the side of the trouble and hit away from it. A fader tees up on the right side of the tee box and aims down the left edge so the ball works back into the middle, and a drawer does the reverse. Where you stand on the tee box also changes how wide the fairway looks, because the angle changes what a corner or a tree line looks like. On a hole that punishes one side, favour the side where a bad shot still leaves you a shot, even if that means a longer approach.

Know when to leave it in the bag. If the driver carries you into the narrowest part of a hole, brings a hazard into reach, or only buys you a slightly shorter approach, a 3-wood or hybrid from the fairway is the better bet. On a par 5 you can't reach, hit the club that leaves your favourite full wedge distance rather than the longest club you own. On a wide hole with room to miss, swing the driver freely and take the yards. A shorter club into the green is still a real advantage, and the driver's the club that gives it to you.

Putting it together

Before every tee shot, ask yourself three things. What's my carry number, which side is the trouble on, and which shape moves the ball away from it? Then set the tee and the ball, aim the face at an intermediate target, and swing with the same tempo you use on the range.

Faults & fixes

Slice

You seeBall starts left of the target and curves hard right, usually high and short.

BecauseThe path travels out to in and the face is open to that path. The arms and shoulders throw the club over the top from the top of the swing, and the face, often because of a weak grip, gets left open.

FixPut a headcover a foot outside the ball on the target line and swing without touching it, feeling the trail elbow drop toward the hip before the hands start down. Then check you can see two and a half knuckles on the grip.

Snap hook

You seeBall starts right of the target, or straight, then dives hard left and runs.

BecauseThe path travels in to out and the face is closed to it by several degrees, usually from a strong grip and a body turn that stalls through impact, which leaves the hands to flip the face shut.

FixKeep the chest turning through impact so the body squares the face instead of the hands, and hold a finish with your belt buckle past the target. If the grip's very strong, weaken the trail hand a fraction.

Pop-up

You seeBall goes almost straight up and lands short, and leaves a mark on the crown of the driver.

BecauseA steep, downward attack angle on a high-teed ball, so the top edge of the face passes under it. Usually the ball is too far back in the stance, or the head slides ahead of it in the downswing.

FixMove the ball forward to the inside of the lead heel, tee it so half the ball sits above the crown, and rehearse a downswing where the head stays behind the ball while the trail shoulder works down rather than out.

Low, spinning bullet

You seeBall starts low, climbs late and stalls in the air with almost no roll.

BecauseA negative attack angle delivered with a lofted face makes a big spin loft and too much backspin. Often the hands are ahead of the ball at impact, which is right for an iron and wrong for the driver.

FixSet up with the hands level with the ball and the spine tilted away from the target, then hit tees only for ten swings, clipping the tee out of the ground on the upswing, before you add a ball.

Heel and toe strikes

You seeContact wanders across the face; heel hits fade weakly and toe hits hook.

BecauseThe distance between the ball and your body changes during the swing, usually because the hips move toward the ball in the downswing or the arms collapse in toward the chest.

FixSpray the face with foot powder, then practise with a tee peg an inch outside the toe and swing so you miss it. Keep your backside back against an imaginary wall through impact.

Drills

01

Tee only

10 swings, then 10 balls, three rounds

An upward attack angle with the head behind the ball.

  1. Tee a ball at normal height and push a second, empty tee into the ground one clubhead ahead of it on the target line, a fraction higher.
  2. Make practice swings that clip the first tee and then brush the second, without touching the ground.
  3. Hit balls with the same feel. The ball should launch high with a flat, quiet flight.
  4. If you hit the ground, move the ball forward an inch and add a little spine tilt away from the target.
02

Headcover gate

3 sets of 10

A neutral or in-to-out path instead of over the top.

  1. Place a headcover a foot outside the ball on the target line, and another a foot inside the ball and behind it.
  2. Swing through the gap. From the top, feel the trail elbow drop toward the hip before the hands move.
  3. Hit balls at 70 percent speed until you clear both headcovers ten times in a row.
  4. Only add speed once the path holds.
03

Step drill

2 sets of 10, then 10 with a normal stance

A transition that starts from the ground, hips before arms.

  1. Set up with your feet together and the ball opposite the lead heel.
  2. Swing back, and as the club nears the top, step the lead foot out to its normal position.
  3. Let the step pull the arms down, hips open, then torso, then club.
  4. Hit balls this way at easy speed, then keep the feeling with a normal stance.
04

Face map

Sets of 10 until 7 of 10 are centred

Centre or slightly high strikes that you've actually found on the face rather than guessed at.

  1. Spray the face with foot powder and hit 10 balls without changing anything.
  2. Read the marks. Heel means you're standing too close or moving toward the ball, and toe means too far away or hanging back.
  3. Adjust the setup by half an inch, respray, and hit 10 more.
  4. Keep going until 7 of 10 sit in the centre or just above it.
