01

Hit down to send it up

An iron sits on the ground, so the club needs to be travelling downward when it reaches the ball. Don't try to help the ball up. The loft on the face takes care of that, and it works best while the club is still descending. On tour a well-struck 7-iron arrives about 4 degrees down, launches around 16 degrees and carries about 172 yards, and it climbed like that because the club was coming down. Try to lift it and you'll move the low point behind the ball, and from there either the leading edge catches the ball thin or the turf gets to the club first.

The thing I want you to hold onto is the low point of your swing arc. With an iron it belongs two to four inches in front of the ball, on the target side. The club meets the ball first while it's still descending, then bottoms out and takes a shallow divot past where the ball was sitting, so it's ball first and turf second. Nearly everything in this lesson, from ball position to weight shift to trajectory control, is really about putting the low point where it belongs, and every thin or fat shot you've ever hit was the low point in the wrong place.

  • You strike an iron on the way down and the loft sends it up
  • The low point sits two to four inches in front of the ball
  • Ball first, then a shallow divot beyond it
  • Thin and fat are the same fault, a low point in the wrong place

02

Setup for a descending strike

Ball position decides where the arc bottoms out, so get this right before anything else. Short irons and wedges go in the centre of the stance. Mid irons, 6 through 8, sit about a ball forward of centre, and long irons and hybrids go another ball forward again. Stance is around shoulder width, measured at the insides of the heels, and you can let it narrow a touch for the wedges. Your hands sit just ahead of the ball, which leans the shaft a few degrees toward the target and points the grip end at the inside of your lead thigh. Left-handers, mirror all of it, so your lead side is the right side and the trail side is the left.

Shaft lean takes care of itself when the hands lead the clubhead into the ball, so don't manufacture it at address, where a few degrees is plenty. Press the hands a long way forward and you de-loft the club and drag the face open. The lean grows on its own at impact, and the club ends up delivering a few degrees less loft than the number stamped on it, which is where that strong, boring flight of a well-struck iron comes from. Set the pressure in your feet at roughly 55 percent on the lead foot, and keep the shoulders close to level, far less tilted than you'd have them with the driver.

  • Wedges in the centre, mid irons a ball forward, long irons a ball further still
  • Hands just ahead, grip end pointing at the inside of the lead thigh
  • About 55 percent of your pressure on the lead foot at address
  • Shoulders closer to level than they are with the driver
03

The turn: coil around a steady centre

With an iron the backswing is a turn, and I mean that literally. Rotate the shoulders around your spine while the head stays close to where it started, and let pressure move to the inside of the trail foot, no further. Once the hips slide away from the target you've swayed, and the low point goes with you, back behind the ball. A feel that works for a lot of my students is the lead shoulder turning down and across toward the ball while the trail hip turns back and around behind you, trail knee still flexed. You'll get all the depth you need from that turn without lifting the arms.

How far back? Far enough that the shoulders have turned around 80 to 90 degrees with the lead arm still reasonably straight, and no further than your body can turn before the arms run on alone. Most golfers I see with contact problems are overswinging, and that extra length costs them their sequence. A three-quarter feeling with a full turn usually gives you the same speed and a much more reliable strike, because the club comes down from somewhere the body can control. Don't worry about the hinge either. The wrists set on their own as the arms pass hip height.

  • Turn around a steady head, with pressure into the inside of the trail foot
  • The hips rotate rather than slide, so no sway
  • A full turn with a three-quarter feel beats an overswing every time
Feel it

Lay a club across your shoulders and turn until the grip end points at the ball or just outside it. That's a full turn with a steady head. If your head has drifted a foot away from the target by the time you get there, you're sliding, not turning.

04

Impact: weight forward, hands ahead

Impact with an iron looks nothing like address, and it shouldn't. By the time the club gets to the ball, pressure has moved hard into the lead foot, 80 percent or more, the hips have opened 35 to 45 degrees toward the target, and your chest is over the ball or just past it. The hands are ahead of the clubhead so the shaft leans forward, the lead wrist is flat or slightly bowed, and the trail wrist has kept its bend. Put those together and you've kept the loft down, the face square and the low point in front of the ball.

All of that gets made in the transition. From the top, the first move is pressure into the lead heel and the lead hip starting to open, before the arms have done anything. That puts the body ahead of the club, which is what lets the hands arrive ahead of the clubhead. If the arms go first the body hangs back, the club catches up early and throws its loft at the ball, and the low point slips behind it. Through impact, keep the chest turning toward the target. Once the body stops turning the hands have no choice but to flip, and a flip adds loft and drags the low point back again.

Your divot tells you the truth. It should start at the ball or just after it, stay shallow, more a strip of bacon than a pork chop, and point at the target or a fraction left of it, because the club travels slightly around the body. If the divot starts behind the ball, that was a fat one, however good the shot felt. No divot at all on a normal lie means the low point never got down to the ground, and that's a thin shot waiting to happen. Practise on grass whenever you can, because mats hide fat strikes by bouncing the club into the ball.

  • 80 percent or more of your pressure on the lead foot at impact
  • Hands ahead of the clubhead, lead wrist flat, trail wrist still bent
  • Keep the chest turning, because a stall makes the hands flip
  • Divot starts at or after the ball, shallow, pointing at the target

05

Distance gapping

A set of irons is built like a ladder. Each club has roughly 3 to 4 degrees more loft and about half an inch less shaft than the one before it, and for most golfers that works out to a gap of about 10 to 15 yards between clubs. A typical mid-handicap male carries his 7-iron somewhere around 140 to 150 yards, so his ladder might run pitching wedge 110, 9-iron 120, 8-iron 132, 7-iron 145, 6-iron 157, 5-iron 168. A tour player's 7-iron carries about 172, and a fast junior or an older golfer could easily sit 30 yards either side of that 145.

Those numbers are examples, not targets. Your gaps depend on your speed, your strike, the ball you play and the lofts on your set, and lofts can vary by 4 or 5 degrees between manufacturers for the same number on the sole. The only honest way to learn your yardages is to measure carry, not total, on a launch monitor or a range with accurate markers. Hit 8 to 10 balls with each club, take the middle of the group and never the best one, and write the numbers on a card. Give me a golfer who knows their 7-iron carries 143 over one who believes it goes 160, every day of the week.

Keep an eye on the top end of the bag as well. Below a certain speed the long irons stop gapping properly, and you'll find a 4-iron and a 5-iron flying the same distance because the ball isn't launching high enough to use the loft. If two clubs carry within 5 yards of each other, swap the longer one for a hybrid. What you want is even spacing, and a hybrid that flies 15 yards further than your 5-iron is doing a lot more for you than a 4-iron that doesn't.

  • Roughly 10 to 15 yards between clubs for most golfers
  • Measure carry rather than total, and take the middle of the group
  • Lofts vary from set to set, so learn your own numbers
  • Once the long irons stop gapping, move to hybrids
06

Controlling trajectory

Trajectory comes down to how much loft you deliver at impact and how fast you deliver it. Move the ball back an inch and the club reaches it earlier in the arc, with more shaft lean and less dynamic loft, so it comes out lower. Move it forward an inch and you get the opposite. Then pair ball position with the length of your finish. A knockdown that stops the follow-through at chest height keeps the hands ahead for longer and the face de-lofted, while a full, high finish lets the loft out. Between them, those two dials cover most of the shots you'll ever need.

Into the wind, take one or two more clubs and swing at 80 percent. Swing harder and you add spin, and the wind grabs a spinning ball and lifts it. Play it an inch back, hands ahead, and finish low with the club pointing at the target, and it'll fly lower with less curve on it. Downwind, or to a raised green, play it slightly forward and finish high. Whatever you do, don't try to hit it higher by leaning back or scooping. That moves the low point behind the ball and hands you exactly the thin shot you were trying to avoid.

  • Ball back and a shorter finish for a lower flight with less spin
  • Ball forward and a full finish for a higher flight
  • Into wind, take more club and swing at 80 percent, never harder
07

Why irons go thin and fat

Thin and fat are the same mistake caught at different moments. Both mean the low point moved behind the ball. When the club bottoms out behind the ball and keeps digging, it hits the ground first and you get the fat one. When it bottoms out behind the ball and is already on its way up by the time it arrives, the leading edge meets the ball's equator and you get the thin one. The four causes I see most are weight hanging on the trail foot, a ball played too far forward, an early release that throws the clubhead past the hands, and a sway in the backswing.

Standing up through impact, what we call early extension, gets you to the same place by a different road. The hips push toward the ball, the spine straightens, and either the arms bend or the club rises. To fix it, keep your backside back at impact and feel the chest stay down over the ball while it turns. All of these causes show up in the divot, so use the line drill below as your first check. If the divot starts behind the line, ask yourself whether the pressure moved forward, whether the ball crept forward and whether the hands led the club in.

On the course

After a fat shot, most golfers I see want to swing easier and lift the next one. Don't. Play it a fraction back in the stance, set 60 percent of your pressure on the lead foot, and commit to taking turf after the ball.

Faults & fixes

Fat

You seeThe club digs in behind the ball, you take a big divot, and the shot comes up 20 yards short.

BecauseThe low point is behind the ball. Usually pressure has stayed on the trail foot through impact, or the hands have released the clubhead early, which adds loft and bottoms the arc out too soon.

FixSet 60 percent of your pressure on the lead foot at address and keep it there through the swing. Then work the line drill until the divot starts in front of the line.

Thin

You seeA low, stinging shot off the ball's equator with no divot. It runs well past the target.

BecauseThe same low point behind the ball, only this time the club is already rising when it gets there. Usually you've stood up out of your posture or you're scooping to help the ball up.

FixKeep your chest down over the ball through impact and feel the trail shoulder working down and through. Pair that with the line drill, and move the ball a fraction back in your stance.

Pull

You seeThe ball starts left of the target and stays fairly straight.

BecauseThe path is out-to-in and the face is square to that path, which makes it closed to the target. You'll see it when the ball is too far forward and the shoulders open up to reach it, or when the arms start the downswing on their own.

FixCheck ball position first. Then rehearse a transition where pressure goes into the lead foot before the arms do anything, and put a headcover just outside the ball so the path can't cut across it.

Push

You seeThe ball starts right of the target and stays out there, often flying lower than usual.

BecauseThe path is in-to-out with the face square to it but open to the target. Usually the body has stalled or slid toward the target, the club drops too far behind you and the face never gets back to square.

FixFeel the chest turn all the way through to face the target, with the lead hip clearing behind you instead of sliding. Hold a full finish, and check the ball hasn't crept too far back in the stance.

Scoop

You seeHigh, weak shots with little or no divot. They fall short and sometimes fade a touch.

BecauseThe trail wrist unbends and the lead wrist cups before impact, which throws loft at the ball and moves the low point back. The hands get to the ball level with the clubhead or behind it.

FixHit punch shots with a nine-to-three swing, holding the lead wrist flat and the trail wrist bent well past impact, and finish with the grip end pointing at your lead hip.

Shank

You seeThe ball comes off the hosel and shoots low and hard to the right.

BecauseThe clubhead comes back to the ball further away from you than it started. Usually the hips have moved toward the ball in the downswing, or the arms have swung out and away from the body.

FixPut a headcover an inch outside the toe and hit balls without touching it. Keep the pressure through the middle of your feet rather than the toes, and keep your backside back through impact.

Drills

01

Line drill

10 swings, then 20 balls

Get the low point in front of the ball.

  1. Spray a straight line on the turf with foot powder or chalk, at right angles to the target line.
  2. Without a ball, make swings where the divot starts on the target side of the line.
  3. Now put a ball on the line and hit it. The divot still needs to start in front of the line.
  4. Hit ten shots and only count the ones where the divot started ahead of the line.

02

Nine to three

3 sets of 10

Compress the ball with a flat lead wrist and the hands ahead.

  1. Take a 9-iron, put the ball in the centre of your stance and set 60 percent of your pressure on the lead foot.
  2. Swing the lead arm back to hip height, nine o'clock, then through to hip height on the other side, three o'clock.
  3. At the finish the shaft should point at the target, with the lead wrist flat and the trail wrist still bent.
  4. Ball first, then turf, at half speed. You're listening for a crisp click, not a thud.

03

Lead foot only

10 balls on the lead foot, then 10 normal

Get your pressure forward at impact instead of hanging back.

  1. Address a ball with a 7-iron, then draw the trail foot back so only the toe is resting on the ground behind you.
  2. Make three-quarter swings with 80 percent of your pressure on the lead foot from start to finish.
  3. Hit ball then turf. If you topple backward, the pressure went to the trail side.
  4. Go back to a normal stance and hold onto that feeling of pressure moving forward before the arms come down.

04

Gapping session

Once a season, and after any equipment change

Honest carry numbers for every iron you own.

  1. On a launch monitor or a range with accurate markers, hit 8 to 10 balls with one club at your normal effort.
  2. Throw out the two best and the two worst, then note the carry of what's left.
  3. Do the same with every iron and hybrid and write the numbers on a card that lives in your bag.
  4. If two clubs carry within 5 yards of each other, change the set or get the lofts adjusted.
