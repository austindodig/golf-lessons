Chipping

A putt with a bit of loft, landed on a spot you picked.

Treat the chip as a putting stroke with some loft on it, pick a landing spot every single time, and use carry-to-roll ratios to choose the club that gets the ball rolling soonest.

01

The chip is a putt with loft

A chip is what you hit when the ball is just off the green and there's nothing but short grass between it and the hole. Its job is to get onto the putting surface as early as it can and then roll like a putt, because a rolling ball is far easier to predict than one that's still in the air. Think of it as a putt that needs a bit of loft to hop the fringe. You make a putting stroke, you read it like a putt, and you aim at a landing spot instead of the hole.

Once you see it that way the technique falls into place. A putt has no wrist hinge, no weight shift and no attempt to lift the ball, and a chip doesn't either. You're not generating any speed, so nothing about the setup needs to be athletic. It's all arranged so the club can brush the grass just past the ball and send it off low and rolling. Most golfers I see chip badly because they're trying to help the ball into the air, when the loft on the club was already doing that job for them.

  • A chip lands early and rolls. A pitch flies most of the way there.
  • Read it like a putt, aim at a landing spot, and let the roll take it the rest of the way.
  • No hinge and no lift. The loft gets the ball airborne for you.
02

Setup: everything forward

Bring the feet close together, heels no more than a few inches apart, and pull the lead foot back a little so the hips are a touch open and can see the target. Put 65 to 70 percent of your weight on the lead foot and leave it there for the whole stroke. Play the ball an inch or two back of centre, roughly opposite the inside of the trail foot, and set the hands ahead of it so they're in front of your lead thigh. You'll find the shaft is now leaning toward the target by 10 to 15 degrees. Left-handers, mirror everything in this lesson.

Grip down to the bottom of the handle, even onto the steel if it's a longer club, and stand closer to the ball than you would for a full shot, so the shaft is more upright and the heel of the club sits slightly off the ground. With the toe down like that the club behaves a lot more like a putter. There's less sole on the grass to snag, and the face swings straight through instead of around you. Use your putting grip if you want to, plenty of good chippers do. Keep the pressure light and the arms soft, hanging from relaxed shoulders.

  • Heels a few inches apart, lead foot pulled back a little.
  • 65–70% of your weight on the lead foot, and it never moves.
  • Ball back of centre, hands in front of the lead thigh, shaft leaning 10–15°.
  • Grip down, stand close, heel of the club just off the ground.
Check

Have a look down at address. A line from your lead shoulder through your hands should reach the ground in front of the ball. If the hands sit behind that line, you've set yourself up to scoop before you've even moved.

03

The stroke: shoulders rock, chest turns, club accelerates

Move the club away with your shoulders, keeping the triangle of arms and chest intact, exactly the way you would for a long putt. The wrists stay quiet. A hint of softness in them is fine, but you should never feel them hinge on purpose or, worse, unhinge. Keep the backswing short, hardly any longer than the putting stroke you'd use for the same total distance. Then let the chest turn through toward the target, so the body pulls the club through impact and the hands stay ahead of the clubhead all the way to the finish.

The low point sits just in front of the ball, so the club hits the ball first and then brushes the grass. You don't have to try for that low point. It comes from the setup, weight forward, hands ahead, and a chest that keeps turning so the arms never stall and flip. Accelerate through the ball. The follow-through should be at least as long as the backswing, with the clubhead staying low to the ground and the face still looking at the target. If the finish stops short, the club slowed down into the ball, and a decelerating club is where both the chunk and the blade come from.

Keep the tempo unhurried without letting it get lazy. Count one on the way back and two through impact, at the rhythm you'd use for a 20-foot putt. Distance comes from the length of the stroke and the club you've picked, never from hitting it harder. If you catch yourself swinging faster for a longer chip, you've got the wrong club in your hands.

  • The shoulders rock the triangle back, wrists quiet, backswing short.
  • The chest turns through and the hands lead the clubhead into the finish.
  • Follow-through at least as long as the backswing, with the clubhead kept low.
  • Same tempo for every chip. Change the club or the length of the stroke, never the speed.

04

Pick a landing spot, then use the ratios

Never aim a chip at the hole. Aim at a landing spot, a patch of green about a yard past the fringe where the ball takes its first bounce on a surface you can trust. Walk over to it, have a look, and picture the roll from there to the hole the same way you'd read a putt. The fringe and the rough bounce however they like, and the green doesn't. Once you've picked the spot, the only question left is which club carries to it and then rolls the rest of the way.

With the shaft leaning forward and the same putting-style stroke, each club gives you a predictable ratio of carry to roll. The classic guide is the rule of 12. Take the club number away from twelve and you've got the parts of roll for every one part of carry. An 8-iron rolls about four parts for each one it carries, a pitching wedge, counted as a 10, rolls about two to one, and a sand wedge, counted as an 11, is roughly one to one. So a chip that needs 5 yards of carry and 20 of roll wants an 8-iron, and 10 and 10 is a sand wedge.

Treat the ratios as a starting point, not a law. A fast, firm green or a downhill run stretches every roll number, and a slow, soft or uphill green shrinks it. Your own delivery matters as well. More shaft lean turns a 9-iron into an 8-iron, and a ball that's sitting down in the grass comes out lower and hotter. Calibrate on the practice green by chipping three clubs to the same landing spot and watching where each one finishes, then adjust the rule of 12 until it's your rule and not somebody else's.

  • Land it about a yard onto the green, never on the fringe.
  • Rule of 12. Twelve minus the club number gives the parts of roll for each part of carry.
  • 8-iron roughly 1:4, pitching wedge roughly 1:2, sand wedge roughly 1:1.
  • Fast, firm or downhill greens add roll, and slow, soft or uphill greens take it away.

05

Club selection: the least loft that lands on the green

Use the lowest lofted club that will still land the ball on the green. Loft adds air time, and air time adds error, because a ball in the air gets affected by the strike, the spin and the lie in ways a rolling ball doesn't. A 7-iron that lands a yard on and rolls 20 yards to the hole is a far easier shot than a lob wedge that has to fly 18 yards and stop inside two. Most chips you'll face have plenty of green to work with, so give yourself permission to pull a mid-iron.

Add loft only when the situation asks for it. That means a short-sided pin with little green to land on, a downhill run that would send an 8-iron chip racing past, rough or a bank between you and the green, or a ball that has to stop within a few paces. Even then, take the least loft that solves the problem. Go through the decision the same way every time. Find the landing spot, estimate the carry and the roll, pick the club whose ratio fits, and rehearse a stroke of the right length beside the ball while you're looking at the spot.

  • The lowest loft that lands on the green. More loft means more error.
  • Add loft only for a short-sided pin, a downhill run, or rough or a bank in the way.
  • The routine is landing spot, carry and roll, club by ratio, then rehearse while looking at the spot.
One club or many?

Some players chip everything with one wedge and vary the landing spot. Others change the club and keep the stroke the same. The second way is easier to learn and easier to repeat under pressure, because the stroke never has to change. Learn it that way first and add the variety later.

06

Reading the lie: tight, fluffy, uphill, downhill

On a tight lie, with the ball sitting on bare or closely mown turf, there's no margin between the ball and the ground, so the club has to hit the ball cleanly or not at all. Keep the shaft lean moderate rather than extreme, because a heavily delofted face raises the trailing edge and lets the leading edge dig in if the strike is a fraction early. Go with a less lofted club, a 7- or 8-iron, keep the weight firmly forward, and make sure the follow-through stays low. Some players even chip with a hybrid from these lies, since the wide sole can't dig and the ball rolls out like a putt.

A fluffy lie, with the ball sitting up on top of the grass, looks like a gift and catches a lot of people out. Ground the club and it sinks into the grass beneath the ball, so the stroke passes underneath, catches the ball high on the face and moves it a few feet. Hover the club level with the ball at address, grip a fraction higher on the handle to match, keep the stroke shallow, and expect less spin and more roll than usual. Out of fluffy grass a sand wedge behaves more like a pitching wedge in the ratios.

On slopes, tilt your shoulders to match the ground and swing along it. An uphill lie adds loft to the club and the slope kills the roll, so take a club or two with less loft, expect the ball to fly higher and shorter, and swing with a bit more length. A downhill lie takes loft off the club and the ground sends the ball running, so take more loft, play the ball a touch further back, keep your weight down the slope, and chase the clubhead down the hill after the ball. Whatever the slope, keep the landing spot on the green and let the ratios sort out the rest.

  • Tight lie means moderate shaft lean, less loft, weight forward and a low follow-through.
  • Fluffy lie means hover the club, keep the stroke shallow and expect more roll.
  • Uphill adds loft and kills roll, so take less loft and swing a bit longer.
  • Downhill takes loft off and adds run, so take more loft and keep your weight down the slope.
07

The bump and run, and the routine that ties it together

The bump and run is the chip taken as far as it'll go. A 7-iron or 8-iron from just off the green or from 20 to 40 yards of firm fairway, with the ball flying a small fraction of the distance and rolling the rest. When the ground between you and the hole is short grass, it's one of the highest percentage shots in golf, because there's almost nothing that can go badly wrong. Set up exactly as you would for a chip, ball back, hands ahead, weight forward, and make a slightly longer putting stroke. The ball comes off low, takes a couple of skips and rolls out like a putt.

Reach for it into the wind, on firm or links-style ground, on an uphill approach with the whole green to work with, and any day your lofted chipping has felt a bit fragile. Read it as one long putt. Judge the pace of the green, allow for the break over the last two thirds of the roll, and pick a landing spot a yard onto the green as usual. The one thing to avoid is a landing spot in long grass or on a slope, because then the first bounce becomes a guess.

Whichever chip you go with, run the same routine. Read the lie first, because it might take a few clubs out of your hands. Find the landing spot and read the roll from there like a putt. Pick the club by ratio, then take two rehearsal strokes beside the ball while you look at the landing spot and feel the length that carries to it. Step in, settle 70 percent of your weight on the lead foot, glance once at the spot, and stroke it with a finish longer than the backswing. Then judge the result by whether you hit the landing spot, not by where the ball ended up.

  • Bump and run means a 7- or 8-iron, the chip setup and a slightly longer putting stroke.
  • Best into wind, on firm ground, uphill, or whenever you've got green to spare.
  • Judge every chip on whether it hit the landing spot.

Faults & fixes

Chunk

You seeThe club digs in behind the ball, the ball hops a few feet, and the divot is behind where the ball was.

BecauseThe low point has moved behind the ball. Most often the weight is sitting on the trail foot, or the wrists unhinge early and the clubhead overtakes the hands.

FixSet 70 percent of your weight on the lead foot with the hands ahead of the lead thigh, and before each chip rehearse brushing the grass an inch in front of the ball three times.

Blade

You seeThe leading edge catches the ball in the middle and it races across the green low and hot.

BecauseThe club is on its way up through impact because the low point is behind the ball. Usually the chest has lifted, or the lead wrist is scooping to help the ball up.

FixKeep the chest down and turning through impact with a flat lead wrist, and hit a few chips with the trail hand only so you feel the club brush the grass after the ball.

Deceleration

You seeContact goes from chunk to blade with the same club, and the follow-through is shorter than the backswing.

BecauseA backswing that's too long for the shot makes you slow the club into the ball, so the low point wanders and the strike turns into a lottery.

FixCut the backswing in half and make the follow-through longer than the backswing on every chip, counting one back and two through at an even tempo.

Scoop and pop-up

You seeThe ball floats up soft and comes down well short of the landing spot.

BecauseThe lead wrist cups through impact and the hands fall behind the clubhead. That adds loft and takes away the forward shaft lean the ratios depend on.

FixHold an alignment stick along the grip so it runs past your lead hip. If it touches your side on the way through, you flipped. Keep chipping until it never touches.

Runs well past the hole

You seeA well-struck chip lands on the green and keeps on rolling a long way past the hole.

BecauseToo little loft for the situation, a flyer from grass trapped between face and ball, or a landing spot that missed the green and caught the firm fringe instead.

FixCheck the landing spot again, land the ball a yard onto the green, and go up one club in loft when the lie is grassy or the green runs away from you downhill.

Drills

01

Landing spot towel

10 balls per club, 3 clubs

Train your eye and your stroke to land the ball on the spot you picked, not somewhere near the hole.

  1. Put a towel or a small hoop down about a yard onto the green from the fringe.
  2. Chip ten balls with a 9-iron, trying to land every one on the towel, and count how many hit it.
  3. Move to a different spot and do the same with a pitching wedge, then with a sand wedge.
  4. Make a note of which club finished closest to the hole from each landing spot.
02

Three clubs, one spot

9 balls per session, twice a week

Work out your own carry-to-roll ratios and check them against the rule of 12.

  1. Pick one landing spot a yard onto the green and one spot off the fringe to chip from.
  2. Chip three balls each with an 8-iron, a pitching wedge and a sand wedge to that spot, with the same stroke every time.
  3. Pace off the roll for each club and see how it compares with 4:1, 2:1 and 1:1.
  4. Write your own ratios on a card and take them out on the course.
03

Alignment stick anti-flip

2 sets of 20

Keep the hands ahead of the clubhead and the shaft leaning forward all the way through impact.

  1. Slide an alignment stick down the grip so it sticks up past your lead hip.
  2. Set up with the weight forward and the hands ahead, so the stick points just outside your lead hip.
  3. Chip 20 balls. If the stick hits your side, the wrists have flipped.
  4. Finish every chip with the stick still pointing away from your body and your chest facing the target.
04

Trail hand only

3 sets of 10

Feel the clubhead brushing the grass after the ball while it stays stable and accelerating.

  1. Hold a pitching wedge in the trail hand only, gripped down to the bottom of the handle.
  2. Set up with your weight forward and the ball back of centre.
  3. Chip ten balls and feel the clubhead brush the grass past the ball while the wrist stays firm.
  4. Put the lead hand back on and hit ten more with the same feel.
