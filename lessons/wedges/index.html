01

A pitch is not a long chip

A chip is a shot that spends most of its journey on the ground: a small stroke, minimal wrist action, and a ball that lands early and rolls like a putt. A pitch is the opposite. It spends most of its journey in the air, carries a hazard, a bank or a run of rough, lands softly and stops within a few paces. Because a pitch flies further, it needs speed, and speed comes from the body turning and the wrists hinging, not from the hands scooping.

The dividing line sits roughly 20 to 30 yards from the hole, or wherever something in your way stops you landing the ball on the green with a chip. From 30 to 100 yards the pitch is your scoring shot: tour players hit it to roughly 16 feet on average from 50 to 75 yards, and a good amateur with three trusted carries per wedge can close a surprising amount of that gap. Own the motion first; the numbers follow from it.

  • Chip: mostly ground, minimal wrists. Pitch: mostly air, body speed and a soft hinge.
  • Pitch whenever you must carry rough, a bank, a bunker or a slope.
  • Your scoring zone is 30 to 100 yards; own three carries per wedge.
02

Setup: narrow, slightly open, leaning on the lead side

Stand with your heels roughly hip-width apart, narrower than your iron stance, so your body can turn freely without swaying. Pull your lead foot back an inch and flare it a little, so your feet and hips sit slightly open, about 15 to 20 degrees left of the target, while your shoulders stay square to the target line. That open lower body gives your hips a head start through the ball, which is what keeps the arms from taking over. Left-handers mirror everything in this lesson.

Play the ball in the centre of your stance for a stock pitch. Set 55 to 60 percent of your weight on your lead foot and keep it there; the pitch has no weight shift to speak of. Your hands sit level with the ball or a touch ahead, the shaft close to vertical, so the wedge keeps its designed loft and bounce. Grip down an inch for control, soften your grip pressure to about four out of ten, and let your arms hang so the club's heel and toe both rest on the turf.

  • Heels hip-width, lead foot pulled back an inch: feet and hips slightly open, shoulders square.
  • Ball centre, 55–60% of weight on the lead foot, and it stays there.
  • Hands level or just ahead, shaft near vertical, grip down an inch.
Check

Look down at address. If the shaft leans hard toward the target, you have turned a 56° wedge into a 48° one and removed most of its bounce. Level the handle and let the loft you paid for reach the ball.

03

The motion: body drives, wrists stay soft

Start the club back with your chest, arms and club moving together as one unit. As the hands pass your trail thigh, let the wrists hinge naturally under the weight of the clubhead; do not set them deliberately. By the time your lead arm reaches nine o'clock, parallel to the ground, the shaft should point roughly at the sky and your chest should have turned about 45 degrees. The feel is a pendulum hung from your sternum: the club swings because the body turns, and the wrists are hinges, not engines.

From the top, the body turns through first and the arms and club follow. Keep the chest rotating toward the target all the way to the finish and the arms will stay in front of you, which keeps the club on plane and the low point just after the ball. Your attack angle should be a modest three to five degrees down, so the club strikes the ball, then brushes a shallow, bacon-strip divot ahead of it. At the finish your chest faces the target, the shaft matches the length of the backswing, and your weight is almost entirely on the lead foot.

Scooping is the pitching killer: the trail wrist unhinges early, the lead wrist cups, the handle stops and the clubhead flips past it. That adds loft, moves the low point behind the ball, and produces the fat-thin lottery that wrecks so many amateur short games. Through impact keep the lead wrist flat and the handle leading the head, your trail palm facing down at the ground. The loft is already on the club. Your only job is to deliver it with the body turning and the hands quiet.

  • Chest, arms and club start back together; wrists hinge from momentum, not effort.
  • Body turns through first; arms stay in front of the chest.
  • Attack angle three to five degrees down; the divot starts after the ball.
  • Lead wrist flat, handle ahead, trail palm down through impact.
Feel it

Hold a wedge across your chest with both arms and make a 9:00 turn back and through. That is the amount of body rotation a stock pitch needs. Now hit balls with the same turn and let the arms simply come along for the ride.

04

The clock system: three stock carries per wedge

Picture yourself standing at the centre of a clock face seen face-on, with your head at twelve and the ball at six. Your lead arm is the hour hand. Swing it back to 7:30, roughly hip high, to 9:00, parallel to the ground, and to 10:30, just above shoulder height, and you have three backswings of fixed length. With the same tempo, the same ball position and the same body turn through, each length produces a repeatable carry. Three positions across four wedges gives you twelve stock numbers, typically five to ten yards apart.

Build your matrix on a range with a laser or a launch monitor, never by guessing. Hit ten balls with your sand wedge at 9:00, discard the two worst, and write down the average carry. Repeat at 7:30 and 10:30, then move through your gap, pitching and lob wedges. A typical result for a male amateur with a 56° wedge is something like 35, 55 and 75 yards, but your numbers are the only ones that matter. Keep the card in your bag and refresh it every couple of months, because tempo drifts.

Two rules make the system hold up under pressure. First, the finish should be at least as long as the backswing; a 9:00 swing finishes at 3:00 or beyond, never short of it, because a shortened finish is deceleration in disguise. Second, keep the tempo identical at every length. Count one to the top and two at impact for every shot, so a 7:30 pitch is a small swing at full rhythm rather than a big swing at half speed.

  • Lead arm is the hour hand: 7:30 hip high, 9:00 parallel, 10:30 above the shoulder.
  • Same tempo and same ball position at every length; only the swing length changes.
  • Ten balls per position, drop the two worst, record the average carry.
  • Finish at least as long as the backswing.

05

Trajectory: low, medium and high with one club

Once your stock carries exist, learn to bend the flight without changing the number. Two things set trajectory: where the ball sits in your stance, and how much loft the face carries at impact. For a low pitch, move the ball an inch back of centre, set your hands an inch further ahead, keep the wrists quieter and finish with the hands low, around hip height. The dynamic loft drops, so the ball launches lower, lands at a shallower angle and runs out after the first bounce. Use it into wind, under branches, or to a back pin with green to work with.

For a high pitch, move the ball an inch forward of centre, open the face a few degrees before you take your grip, and let the club release fully into a tall finish with the hands high. The extra loft launches the ball steeply so it lands soft and stops quickly, ideal over a bunker to a tight pin. Two cautions. The face controls where the ball starts, so an open face sends it right of your stance line; aim your feet and hips a little further left to compensate. The higher flight also costs carry, so take the next clock position up, or one more club.

  • Low: ball an inch back, hands ahead, quiet wrists, hands finish hip high.
  • High: ball an inch forward, face opened before gripping, full release, tall finish.
  • An open face starts the ball right; aim the body further left to match.
  • Height costs carry: add a clock position or take one more club.

06

Spin comes from friction, not from effort

Backspin is made by friction between the face and the cover of the ball during the half a millisecond they are in contact. Everything that raises friction raises spin: clean, sharp grooves, a dry face, a dry ball, and a strike that pinches the ball against the turf before the club touches grass. Everything that lowers friction lowers spin: wet grass, mud, a worn face, and any blade of grass trapped between face and ball. That last one is the flyer, a shot that launches higher, spins less and sails ten yards past your number.

Ball-first contact is the non-negotiable. Your low point must sit an inch or two past the ball, which everything above is designed to produce: weight lead side, handle ahead, chest turning through. A useful idea is spin loft, the gap between the loft the face delivers and your attack angle. A 56° wedge delivered at 50° with a four degree descending blow has a spin loft of 54°; wedge spin peaks somewhere in the mid forties. Push past that and the face slides under the ball instead of gripping it, which is why a scooped strike floats high and spins less than a compressed one.

Look after the face. Wipe it with a damp towel before every wedge shot, and keep a groove brush on the bag, because a dirty face on a wet day can cut your spin dramatically. Grooves also wear: after a couple of hundred rounds a wedge that once checked on the second bounce will spin noticeably less, so consider replacing the wedges you use most every few seasons. On the course the honest test is simple: if your ball routinely runs past pins you thought you had covered, suspect the lie or the face before you suspect the swing.

  • Spin is friction: clean grooves, dry face, dry ball, ball before turf.
  • Grass between face and ball is a flyer: higher, less spin, longer.
  • Wipe the face before every wedge shot; brush the grooves every week.
07

Reading the lie before you pick the shot

Every pitch begins with a look at how the ball sits, because the lie decides how much of the technique above you are allowed to use. From a clean fairway lie everything is available: any trajectory, full spin, a stock number. From a tight lie on firm, bare turf, keep the face square rather than open, so the leading edge sits close to the ground, and favour the low or medium flight; an open face on a tight lie raises the leading edge and invites a thin. Commit to ball-first contact and accept a lower, running flight.

From light rough expect a flyer: grass between face and ball kills spin, so the ball launches higher, releases further, and often carries a few yards longer than your card says. Land it short of the number and let it run. From thick rough the club must cut through grass, so play the ball a touch back, hinge the wrists earlier for a steeper descent, grip firmer with the lead hand so the hosel cannot twist shut, and swing with more speed than the distance suggests. It will come out with little spin, so land it on the front of the green and let it chase.

Sitting up in fluffy rough looks inviting, but the ball can slide under the face and go nowhere, so hover the club at address, keep the face square and swing a fraction shallower. On slopes, set your shoulders parallel to the ground you stand on and swing along it: uphill adds loft and costs carry, downhill subtracts loft and adds run. Then run the same routine every time: read the lie, pick a landing spot, choose the club and clock position that carries to it, rehearse the length with two practice swings, and commit.

  • Tight lie: square face, low or medium flight, ball first.
  • Light rough: expect a flyer, land it short of your number.
  • Thick rough: ball back, earlier hinge, firm lead hand, extra speed.
  • Slopes: shoulders match the ground; uphill adds loft, downhill adds run.
On the course

Your best pitch is rarely the prettiest one. A 9:00 gap wedge that lands on your number and releases four yards beats a 10:30 lob wedge that has to be perfect. Choose the shot with the widest margin for error and give it a full, confident finish.

Faults & fixes

Fat (chunk)

You seeThe club digs behind the ball, takes a deep divot and the ball flies half the distance.

BecauseThe low point has moved behind the ball, usually because weight drifted to the trail foot or the wrists released early and the clubhead overtook the hands.

FixSet 60 percent of your weight on the lead foot and keep it there, then hit 9:00 pitches with a towel laid six inches behind the ball; the towel must stay untouched.

Thin (blade)

You seeThe leading edge catches the ball at the equator and it screams low across the green.

BecauseThe same low-point fault as the chunk, seen from the other side: the club bottomed out behind the ball and is rising through impact, often because the chest lifts or the wrists flip.

FixKeep your chest down and turning over the ball through impact with the hands ahead, and rehearse brushing the turf an inch in front of the ball before every shot.

Scoop (high floater)

You seeThe ball pops up high with little spin and lands well short, and you feel the clubhead pass your hands.

BecauseThe trail wrist unhinges early and the lead wrist cups, which adds loft, moves the low point back and reduces compression.

FixHit 7:30 pitches to a 20-yard target with a flat lead wrist and the trail palm facing the ground, holding the finish with the handle still ahead of the clubhead.

Inconsistent carry

You seeThe same club and the same swing carry 55 yards one time and 45 the next.

BecauseBackswing length or tempo varies from shot to shot, or the finish shortens and the club decelerates through the ball.

FixUse the clock positions with an identical two-count tempo and a finish at least as long as the backswing; check your lead-arm position in a mirror or on video.

High shot starts right

You seeYou open the face for a soft, high pitch and the ball starts right of the target and stays there.

BecauseThe face controls start direction, so an opened face that is not matched by a body aimed further left sends the start line right of the target.

FixOpen the face first, then take your grip, then aim your feet and hips left of the target until the face points at your landing spot.

Shank

You seeThe ball rockets low and right off the hosel.

BecauseThe hands and clubhead move away from the body through impact, usually because the arms disconnect from the chest or the weight tips onto the toes.

FixPlace a second ball or a headcover an inch outside the target ball and hit 9:00 pitches that miss it; keep your weight through the arches and the arms in front of the chest.

Drills

01

Clock calibration

30 balls per wedge, one wedge per session

Measure your three stock carries for every wedge and build a yardage card you can trust.

  1. Warm up with ten stock pitches, then set a laser or launch monitor on a flat target.
  2. Hit ten balls at 9:00 with your sand wedge, note each carry, and drop the two worst.
  3. Average the remaining eight and write the number down; repeat at 7:30 and 10:30.
  4. Work through your gap, pitching and lob wedges the same way.
  5. Transfer the twelve numbers to a card in your bag and refresh them every couple of months.
02

Towel low point

3 sets of 10

Train ball-first contact and a low point in front of the ball.

  1. Lay a folded towel on the grass six inches behind your ball.
  2. Set up with 60 percent of your weight on the lead foot and hands slightly ahead.
  3. Hit 9:00 pitches; the towel must stay untouched and the divot must begin at or after the ball.
  4. If you hit the towel, feel the chest turning over the ball through impact and try again.
03

Connection glove

2 sets of 10 at each length

Keep the arms and body moving together so the body, not the hands, powers the pitch.

  1. Tuck a glove or headcover under your lead armpit.
  2. Hit 7:30 pitches, then 9:00 pitches, keeping the glove pinned until the finish.
  3. If the glove drops, the arms have separated from the chest; shorten the swing and rebuild the feel.
  4. Finish every rep with your chest facing the target and the glove still in place.
04

Trajectory ladder

5 ladders per club

Hit low, medium and high pitches to the same target with one club.

  1. Pick a 50-yard target and set three balls in a row.
  2. Ball one: an inch back, hands ahead, hands finish hip high. Ball two: stock. Ball three: an inch forward, face open, tall finish.
  3. Adjust the swing length so all three finish pin high, and note the extra length the high shot needs.
  4. Repeat with your gap wedge to a 65-yard target.
