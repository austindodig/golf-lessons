01

A pitch is not a long chip

A chip spends most of its life on the ground. It's a small stroke with hardly any wrist in it, and the ball lands early and rolls out like a putt. A pitch is mostly airborne. It carries a hazard, a bank or a stretch of rough, then lands softly and stops within a few paces. Flying that far takes speed, and the speed has to come from the body turning and the wrists hinging. You won't get any of it by scooping with the hands.

The dividing line is roughly 20 to 30 yards from the hole, or wherever something in the way stops you landing a chip on the green. From 30 to 100 yards the pitch is your scoring shot. Tour players hit it to about 16 feet on average from 50 to 75 yards, and a decent amateur with three trusted carries per wedge can close more of that gap than you'd think. Get the motion right first and the numbers will follow on from it.

  • A chip is mostly ground and hardly any wrist. A pitch is mostly air, driven by body speed and a soft hinge.
  • Pitch whenever there's rough, a bank, a bunker or a slope to carry.
  • Your scoring zone runs from 30 to 100 yards, so learn three carries for each wedge.
02

Setup: narrow, slightly open, leaning on the lead side

Set your heels roughly hip-width apart, narrower than you'd stand for an iron, so the body can turn freely without swaying. Pull the lead foot back an inch and flare it a little. That puts your feet and hips slightly open, about 15 to 20 degrees left of the target, while the shoulders stay square to the target line. An open lower body gives the hips a head start through the ball, and that head start is what stops the arms taking over. Left-handers, mirror everything in this lesson.

For a stock pitch, the ball goes in the centre of your stance. Put 55 to 60 percent of your weight on the lead foot and leave it there, because there's no real weight shift in a pitch. Your hands sit level with the ball or a touch ahead and the shaft is close to vertical, which lets the wedge keep the loft and bounce it was built with. Grip down an inch for control, soften your grip pressure to about four out of ten, and let the arms hang until the heel and toe of the club are both resting on the turf.

  • Heels hip-width apart, lead foot back an inch, feet and hips slightly open with the shoulders square.
  • Ball in the centre, 55–60% of your weight on the lead foot, and it doesn't move.
  • Hands level with the ball or just ahead, shaft near vertical, grip down an inch.
Check

Have a look down at address. If the shaft is leaning hard toward the target, you've just turned a 56° wedge into a 48° one and taken most of its bounce away. Stand the handle up and let the loft you paid for reach the ball.

03

The motion: body drives, wrists stay soft

Start the club back with your chest, arms and club all moving together in one piece. As the hands pass the trail thigh, let the wrists hinge on their own under the weight of the clubhead. Don't set them deliberately. By the time the lead arm gets to nine o'clock, parallel to the ground, the shaft should be pointing roughly at the sky and your chest should have turned about 45 degrees. I like the picture of a pendulum hanging from your sternum. The club swings because the body turns, and the wrists are just hinges along the way.

From the top, the body turns through first and the arms and club come along behind it. Keep the chest rotating toward the target all the way to the finish and the arms will stay in front of you, which keeps the club on plane and the low point just past the ball. You're after a modest attack angle, three to five degrees down, so the club strikes the ball and then brushes a shallow, bacon-strip divot ahead of it. At the finish your chest faces the target, the shaft has gone through about as far as it went back, and nearly all of your weight is on the lead foot.

Scooping ruins more amateur pitching than anything else I see. The trail wrist unhinges early, the lead wrist cups, the handle stalls and the clubhead flips past it. That adds loft, moves the low point behind the ball, and gives you the fat-or-thin lottery that wrecks so many short games. Through impact keep the lead wrist flat and the handle leading the head, with your trail palm facing down at the ground. The loft is already built into the club. Your job is to deliver it with the body turning and the hands quiet.

  • Chest, arms and club start back together, and the wrists hinge from momentum, not effort.
  • The body turns through first, with the arms staying in front of the chest.
  • Three to five degrees down, and the divot starts after the ball.
  • Lead wrist flat, handle ahead and trail palm facing down through impact.
Feel it

Hold a wedge across your chest with both arms and make a 9:00 turn back and through. That's the amount of body rotation a stock pitch needs. Now hit some balls with that same turn and let the arms come along for the ride.

04

The clock system: three stock carries per wedge

Picture yourself standing in the middle of a clock face viewed from the front, head at twelve, ball at six. Your lead arm is the hour hand. Swing it back to 7:30, roughly hip high, to 9:00, parallel to the ground, or to 10:30, just above shoulder height, and you've got three backswings of a fixed length. Keep the tempo, the ball position and the body turn through the same, and each length gives you a carry you can repeat. Three positions across four wedges makes twelve stock numbers, usually five to ten yards apart.

Build the matrix on a range with a laser or a launch monitor. Don't guess at it. Hit ten balls with your sand wedge at 9:00, throw out the two worst and write down the average carry. Do the same at 7:30 and 10:30, then work through the gap, pitching and lob wedges. A typical result for a male amateur with a 56° wedge is something like 35, 55 and 75 yards, but honestly, your own numbers are the only ones that matter. Keep the card in your bag and refresh it every couple of months, because tempo drifts over time.

Two rules keep the system honest when you're under pressure. First, the finish should be at least as long as the backswing, so a 9:00 swing finishes at 3:00 or beyond and never short of it. A finish that gets cut off is deceleration in disguise. Second, keep the tempo the same at every length. Count one to the top and two at impact on every shot, so a 7:30 pitch is a small swing at full rhythm, not a big swing at half speed.

  • Your lead arm is the hour hand. 7:30 is hip high, 9:00 is parallel to the ground, 10:30 is just above the shoulder.
  • Same tempo and ball position at every length. The only thing that changes is how far back you go.
  • Ten balls at each position, drop the two worst, write down the average carry.
  • The finish is at least as long as the backswing.

05

Trajectory: low, medium and high with one club

Once you've got your stock carries, learn to bend the flight without changing the number. Trajectory comes from where the ball sits in your stance and how much loft the face is carrying at impact. For a low pitch, move the ball an inch back of centre, set the hands an inch further ahead, keep the wrists quieter and finish with the hands low, around hip height. Dynamic loft drops, so the ball launches lower, lands at a shallower angle and runs out after the first bounce. Use it into wind, under branches, or to a back pin when there's green to work with.

For a high pitch, move the ball an inch forward of centre, open the face a few degrees before you take your grip, and let the club release fully into a tall finish with the hands high. The extra loft launches the ball steeply, so it lands soft and stops fast, which is exactly what you want over a bunker to a tight pin. Two things to watch with this one. The face controls where the ball starts, so an open face sends it right of your stance line and you'll need to aim your feet and hips a little further left to allow for it. And the higher flight costs you carry, so go up a clock position or take one more club.

  • Low pitch means ball an inch back, hands ahead, quiet wrists and a hip-high finish.
  • High pitch means ball an inch forward, face opened before you grip, full release and a tall finish.
  • An open face starts the ball right, so aim the body further left to match it.
  • Height costs carry, so go up a clock position or take one more club.

06

Spin comes from friction, not from effort

Backspin is friction between the face and the cover of the ball during the half a millisecond they're in contact. Whatever raises friction raises spin, which means clean, sharp grooves, a dry face, a dry ball, and a strike that pinches the ball against the turf before the club touches grass. Wet grass, mud, a worn face and any blade of grass caught between face and ball all bring it down. That last one is the flyer, the shot that launches higher, spins less and sails ten yards past your number.

Ball-first contact is the one thing you can't do without. Your low point needs to sit an inch or two past the ball, and everything above, the weight on the lead side, the handle ahead, the chest turning through, is there to produce it. It's worth knowing about spin loft too. That's the gap between the loft the face delivers and your attack angle. A 56° wedge delivered at 50° with a four degree descending blow has a spin loft of 54°, and wedge spin peaks somewhere in the mid forties. Go past that and the face slides under the ball instead of gripping it, which is exactly why a scooped strike floats high and spins less than a compressed one.

Look after the face. Wipe it with a damp towel before every wedge shot and keep a groove brush on the bag, because a dirty face on a wet day can cut your spin dramatically. Grooves wear out as well. After a couple of hundred rounds, a wedge that used to check on the second bounce will be spinning noticeably less, so think about replacing the wedges you use most every few seasons. On the course, if the ball keeps running past pins you thought you had covered, suspect the lie or the face before you start blaming the swing.

  • Spin is friction. Clean grooves, a dry face, a dry ball, and ball before turf.
  • Grass between face and ball gives you a flyer, which launches higher, spins less and goes longer.
  • Wipe the face before every wedge shot and brush the grooves out every week.
07

Reading the lie before you pick the shot

Every pitch starts with a look at how the ball is sitting, because the lie decides how much of the technique above you're allowed to use. From a clean fairway lie you can have the lot, any trajectory, full spin, a stock number. From a tight lie on firm, bare turf, keep the face square rather than open so the leading edge sits close to the ground, and favour the low or medium flight. Open the face on a tight lie and you lift the leading edge and invite a thin. Commit to ball-first contact and accept that it'll come out lower and run.

From light rough, expect a flyer. Grass between the face and the ball kills the spin, so it launches higher, releases further and often carries a few yards longer than your card says. Land it short of the number and let it run. From thick rough the club has to cut through grass first, so play the ball a touch back, hinge the wrists earlier for a steeper descent, grip a bit firmer with the lead hand so the hosel can't twist shut, and swing with more speed than the distance suggests. It'll come out with very little spin on it, so land it on the front of the green and let it chase.

A ball sitting up in fluffy rough looks inviting, but the club can slide right under it and the ball goes nowhere, so hover the club at address, keep the face square and swing a fraction shallower. On slopes, set your shoulders parallel to the ground you're standing on and swing along it. Uphill adds loft and costs carry, and downhill takes loft away and adds run. Then go through the same routine every time. Read the lie, pick a landing spot, choose the club and clock position that carries to it, rehearse the length with a couple of practice swings, and commit to it.

  • Tight lie means a square face, a low or medium flight, and ball first.
  • Light rough means a flyer, so land it short of your number.
  • Thick rough means ball back, an earlier hinge, a firm lead hand and extra speed.
  • On slopes, match your shoulders to the ground. Uphill adds loft, downhill adds run.
On the course

The best pitch is hardly ever the prettiest one. A 9:00 gap wedge that lands on your number and releases four yards will beat a 10:30 lob wedge that has to be perfect, every time. Pick the shot with the most room for error and give it a full, confident finish.

Faults & fixes

Fat (chunk)

You seeThe club digs in behind the ball, takes a deep divot, and the ball goes about half the distance.

BecauseThe low point has moved behind the ball. Usually the weight has drifted onto the trail foot, or the wrists have released early and the clubhead has overtaken the hands.

FixSet 60 percent of your weight on the lead foot and keep it there, then hit 9:00 pitches with a towel laid six inches behind the ball. You're not allowed to touch the towel.

Thin (blade)

You seeThe leading edge catches the ball on its equator and it screams low across the green.

BecauseThe same low-point fault as the chunk, just seen from the other side. The club has bottomed out behind the ball and is on its way up through impact, often because the chest lifts or the wrists flip.

FixKeep your chest down and turning over the ball through impact with the hands ahead, and before every shot rehearse brushing the turf an inch in front of the ball.

Scoop (high floater)

You seeThe ball pops up high with hardly any spin and lands well short. You can feel the clubhead passing your hands.

BecauseThe trail wrist unhinges early and the lead wrist cups. That adds loft, moves the low point back and takes all the compression out of the strike.

FixHit 7:30 pitches to a 20-yard target with a flat lead wrist and the trail palm facing the ground, then hold the finish with the handle still ahead of the clubhead.

Inconsistent carry

You seeThe same club and what feels like the same swing carries 55 yards one time and 45 the next.

BecauseBackswing length or tempo is changing from shot to shot, or the finish is getting shorter and the club decelerates into the ball.

FixUse the clock positions with the same two-count tempo and a finish at least as long as the backswing, and check where your lead arm actually gets to in a mirror or on video.

High shot starts right

You seeYou open the face for a soft, high pitch and the ball starts right of the target and never comes back.

BecauseThe face controls where the ball starts. Open it without aiming the body further left and the ball starts right of the target.

FixOpen the face first, then take your grip, then aim the feet and hips left of the target until the face is pointing at your landing spot.

Shank

You seeThe ball comes off the hosel and rockets low and right.

BecauseThe hands and clubhead move away from the body through impact, usually because the arms have come off the chest or the weight has tipped onto the toes.

FixPut a second ball or a headcover an inch outside the one you're hitting and play 9:00 pitches that miss it. Keep your weight through the arches of your feet and the arms in front of the chest.

Drills

01

Clock calibration

30 balls per wedge, one wedge per session

Measure three stock carries for every wedge and build a yardage card you can actually trust.

  1. Warm up with ten stock pitches, then set up a laser or launch monitor on a flat target.
  2. Hit ten balls at 9:00 with your sand wedge, note every carry, and drop the two worst.
  3. Average the eight that are left and write the number down, then do the same at 7:30 and 10:30.
  4. Work through the gap, pitching and lob wedges the same way.
  5. Put all twelve numbers on a card in your bag and refresh them every couple of months.

02

Towel low point

3 sets of 10

Groove ball-first contact with the low point in front of the ball.

  1. Lay a folded towel on the grass six inches behind the ball.
  2. Set up with 60 percent of your weight on the lead foot and your hands slightly ahead.
  3. Hit 9:00 pitches. The towel stays untouched and the divot starts at the ball or after it.
  4. If you catch the towel, feel the chest turning over the ball through impact and have another go.

03

Connection glove

2 sets of 10 at each length

Keep the arms and body moving together, so the body powers the pitch instead of the hands.

  1. Tuck a glove or a headcover under your lead armpit.
  2. Hit 7:30 pitches, then 9:00 pitches, and keep the glove pinned there right through to the finish.
  3. If the glove drops, the arms have come away from the chest. Shorten the swing and build the feel back up.
  4. Finish every rep with your chest facing the target and the glove still where you put it.

04

Trajectory ladder

5 ladders per club

Hit low, medium and high pitches to one target with the same club.

  1. Pick a 50-yard target and line up three balls.
  2. Play the first ball an inch back with the hands ahead and a hip-high finish, the second one stock, and the third an inch forward with the face open and a tall finish.
  3. Adjust swing length until all three finish pin high, and take note of how much extra length the high one needs.
  4. Do it again with your gap wedge to a 65-yard target.
